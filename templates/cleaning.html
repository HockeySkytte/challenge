Data Cleaning
What happens to the raw file, in ten steps
  1. 1Clean the teams

    The three team columns are renamed to home_team, away_team and team, which is easier to work with than the spaced-out names the file uses.

    Olympic team names are shortened to just the nation, because “Olympic (Women)” is the same for all of them and tells you nothing. Olympic (Women) - Canada becomes Canada, and Olympic Athletes from Russia becomes Russia. The two college teams keep their full names.

  2. 2Turn the clock into a running total

    The clock counts down from 20:00 and restarts every period, so it cannot be used to ask what happened next. It is replaced with time_elapsed: the number of seconds since the game started. Period 1 runs 0 to 1,200, period 2 runs 1,200 to 2,400, and so on. The column that held the period is now period.

  3. 3Describe the game from the eventing team's point of view

    Three new columns say who took the event and what the game looked like at that moment. All three are written from the point of view of the team that took the event, so one value means the same thing for both teams:

    • venue — Home or Away.
    • strength_state — skaters for and against, such as 5v4. So 5v4 is always a power play for the team that took the event, whichever team that is.
    • score_state — the goal difference. +1 means the team that took the event was winning by one.

    The four columns these came from only read correctly for the home team, so they are removed.

  4. 4Name the shot outcome, and give the receiver a row

    Every shot was called Shot, with the outcome in a separate column. The outcome is now part of the name — Shot on Net, Missed Shot or Blocked Shot — so a shot says what happened to it. Goals keep the name Goal.

    A Play is a pass that reached its target. The file credited only the passer, which left the receiver invisible. A Reception row is now added underneath every pass, for the player who received it, at the place they received it. This is why the cleaned file has {{ "{:,}".format(receptions) }} more rows than the raw one.

    The remaining column names are tidied up too, to player, player_2, event and detail_1 to detail_4.

  5. 5Put the middle of the rink at zero

    The coordinates are renamed to x1, y1 for where the event happened and x2, y2 for where a pass was aimed.

    They are also shifted so the middle of the rink is zero: x runs from −100 at your own goal line to +100 at the goal you are attacking, and y runs −42.5 to +42.5 across the ice. Positive x is always the attacking end, whichever team took the event.

  6. 6Find which rink box each event is in

    The rink is divided into 58 boxes. boxid is the box an event happened in and boxid_2 is the box a pass was aimed at.

    Two edge cases need a rule. An event sitting exactly on a blue line counts as being in the end zone rather than the middle, because the blue line is where the end zone begins. An event logged right on the boards, or out in a rounded corner, is put in the nearest box — a puck is never off the ice.

  7. 7Mark where a possession starts

    possession_start is 1 when an event is the moment a player takes control of the puck — a Puck Recovery, a Reception or a Takeaway — and 0 for everything else.

  8. 8Build the expected-goal models

    Two expected-goal (xG) models are built, both simple logistic regressions. They look at only four things: how far the shot was from the net, how much of the goal mouth the shooter could see, whether there was traffic in front, and whether it was a one-timer.

    xg_all_shots is trained on every shot attempt. xg_on_net is trained only on shots that reached the net. Both are trained on the international games at 5v5, and only those rows are given a value — the models say nothing about a college game or a power play.

  9. 9Label the competition

    competition says which tournament each game belongs to: Olympic, NCAA, Rivalry Series or World Championship.

  10. 10Count the next 15 seconds

    Every possession start and every Zone Entry opens a 15-second window. Everything that happens inside it is counted twice, once for the team that opened the window and once for the other team:

    • CF_15 / CA_15 — every shot attempt, on net, missed, blocked or a goal.
    • FF_15 / FA_15 — the same, but without the blocked ones.
    • SF_15 / SA_15 — shots that reached the net.
    • GF_15 / GA_15 — goals.
    • xGF_15 / xGA_15 — expected goals.

    A goal, or the end of a period, closes the window early. A goal still counts, because it happened inside the window.

Data concerns

Three things this file cannot tell us. None of them break anything, but they are worth knowing before reading a number on the other pages.

  • The sample is small. {{ totals.games }} games, {{ totals.players }} players and {{ totals.goals }} goals in the whole file, with {{ "{:,}".format(totals.shot_attempts) }} shot attempts to look at. Both xG models are fitted on the same {{ totals.training_goals }} goals — the ones scored at 5v5 in the international games. Every breakdown thins out from there: of the {{ "{:,}".format(totals.windows) }} possession windows behind the Possession Values page, only {{ "{:,}".format(totals.windows_with_shot) }} contain a shot attempt at all. Read the shape of a map rather than its exact figures, and be careful about leaning on any single region.
  • A possession has a beginning, but no recorded end. The file marks where and when a player's possession starts — a Puck Recovery, a Reception or a Takeaway — but nothing marks where or when it ends. There is no possession-end event, and the nearest proxy, the moment the other team next gains the puck, cannot tell a clean possession from one interrupted by a whistle.
    So possession time cannot be measured, and neither can carry distance. Only passes carry a second point, where the puck was aimed, which is why a pass has a length on the Event Map and a carry does not: we know where the carrier was when the event was logged, never where they picked the puck up.
  • There is no shift data. The file records how many skaters were on the ice for each event — that is where strength_state comes from, and it is what makes the 5v5 filtering on the other pages possible — but not who they were. Only the players directly involved in an event are ever named.
    That rules out on-ice analytics entirely. Anything of the form "how did this player's team do while he was on the ice" — on-ice corsi, on-ice expected goals, line combinations, deployment — cannot be built from this file, because the other skaters are anonymous.
